Genetics is the branch of biology concerned with heredity and genetic variation in living organisms. The modern science of genetics traces its origins to the work of Gregor Mendel, who formulated the laws of inheritance through experiments with garden peas in the 1860s. Mendel's law of segregation states that alleles separate during gamete formation, while his law of independent assortment describes how genes for different traits are inherited independently when located on different chromosomes.

DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) serves as the molecular carrier of genetic information in most living organisms. The double helix structure of DNA, elucidated by Watson and Crick in 1953, explained how genetic information is stored and replicated. Each strand of the double helix serves as a template for synthesising a complementary strand, ensuring faithful copying during cell division. The sequence of nucleotide bases — adenine, thymine, guanine, and cytosine — encodes genetic instructions.

Gene expression involves transcription and translation. During transcription, RNA polymerase synthesises messenger RNA (mRNA) from a DNA template. During translation, ribosomes decode the mRNA sequence to assemble a specific protein according to the genetic code. Each three-nucleotide codon specifies one of twenty amino acids or a stop signal. Regulatory sequences in the DNA control when, where, and how much of each protein is produced.

Mutations — changes in the DNA sequence — are the ultimate source of genetic variation. Point mutations change individual nucleotides; insertions and deletions shift the reading frame. Most mutations are neutral or harmful, but rare beneficial mutations can spread through a population by natural selection. Chromosomal rearrangements, including duplications and inversions, also generate variation. Horizontal gene transfer in bacteria accelerates the acquisition of advantageous traits such as antibiotic resistance.

Modern genomics applies high-throughput sequencing to analyse entire genomes. Genome-wide association studies identify genetic variants linked to complex diseases such as diabetes, heart disease, and schizophrenia. CRISPR-Cas9 technology enables precise editing of DNA sequences, offering potential therapies for genetic disorders and new tools for agricultural improvement. Ethical questions about germline editing and genetic privacy remain central to public debates about the future of genomics.
