Differentiated instruction is a pedagogical approach in which teachers adjust content, process, and product to meet the diverse learning needs, interests, and readiness levels of individual students within the same classroom. Developed and popularised by Carol Ann Tomlinson, this framework challenges the assumption that all students learn the same material in the same way at the same pace. Differentiation is not about having different expectations for different students; rather, it provides multiple pathways to reach the same high-quality learning goals.

Formative assessment is the cornerstone of effective differentiation. Teachers use quizzes, exit tickets, observations, and discussions to gather ongoing data about student understanding. This data informs instructional decisions: which students need additional scaffolding, which are ready for extension activities, and which misconceptions must be addressed. Unlike summative assessment, formative feedback is intended to adjust teaching while learning is still in progress.

Flexible grouping is a practical strategy within differentiated classrooms. Students may work in ability-based groups for targeted skill instruction, in mixed-ability groups to benefit from peer teaching, or individually for self-paced tasks. The key principle is that groupings remain fluid and purposeful, not fixed by perceived ability. Labelling students as permanently high or low achievers undermines motivation and reinforces inequality, so grouping arrangements should change regularly based on current assessment data.

Learning environment design supports differentiation by providing students with choices and clear expectations. Learning stations or centres allow students to rotate through tasks matched to their readiness or interest. Anchor activities — tasks that students engage in independently when they finish primary work — prevent faster learners from disengaging. Digital tools such as adaptive learning platforms can further personalise practice by adjusting difficulty in real time based on student responses.

The teacher's role shifts in a differentiated classroom from knowledge transmitter to learning facilitator. This requires ongoing professional development, strong content knowledge, and skill in interpreting assessment data. Whole-class instruction remains valuable for building shared knowledge, but teachers must also manage small-group instruction and individual conferences simultaneously. Research consistently shows that well-implemented differentiation improves outcomes for students at all ability levels when combined with high academic expectations for every learner.
