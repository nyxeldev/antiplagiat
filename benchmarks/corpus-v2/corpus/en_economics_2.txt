Market economies are economic systems in which decisions about production, distribution, and pricing are determined primarily through the interactions of buyers and sellers in competitive markets, rather than by central planning. The price mechanism serves as the core coordinating device: rising prices signal scarcity and attract additional supply, while falling prices indicate surplus and discourage further production. Adam Smith described this spontaneous coordination as an "invisible hand" guiding resources toward their most valued uses.

Competition among producers drives innovation, cost reduction, and quality improvement. When multiple firms compete for the same customers, each has an incentive to offer better products at lower prices. Perfect competition, where many sellers offer identical products, represents a theoretical benchmark; real markets exhibit varying degrees of competition. Monopolies and oligopolies can restrict output and raise prices, generating economic inefficiency that may justify regulatory intervention.

Market failures occur when competitive markets fail to allocate resources efficiently. Public goods — such as national defence and public health infrastructure — are non-excludable and non-rival, meaning markets underprovide them because free-riding is possible. Externalities arise when production or consumption imposes costs or benefits on third parties not reflected in market prices; pollution is a classic negative externality. Information asymmetries, as analysed by Akerlof, Spence, and Stiglitz, can lead to market breakdowns in insurance and credit markets.

Financial markets channel savings into productive investment by bringing together borrowers and lenders, equity issuers and investors. Stock markets provide firms with access to long-term capital and investors with ownership stakes and dividend income. Bond markets enable governments and corporations to borrow at varying maturities. Financial intermediaries such as banks reduce transaction costs by pooling deposits and screening borrowers. The stability of financial systems is a public good requiring prudential regulation and supervision.

Globalisation has deepened market integration across national boundaries. Trade in goods and services allows countries to specialise according to comparative advantage, raising aggregate productivity. Foreign direct investment transfers technology and management practices across borders. However, globalisation also exposes domestic markets to external shocks and raises distributional questions about the sharing of gains from trade. Policy debates about trade agreements, capital account openness, and industrial policy reflect these tensions between efficiency and equity.
