Medieval Islamic philosophy represents a remarkable synthesis of ancient Greek thought and Islamic theology, enriched by original thinkers from the Middle East and Central Asia. Al-Kindi, the first Arab philosopher, introduced the philosophical method into Arabic culture and translated works of Aristotle. Al-Farabi systematised logic, metaphysics, and political philosophy, developing a doctrine of the ideal polity and a hierarchy of intellects that influenced subsequent thinkers.

Ibn Sina (Avicenna) constructed a comprehensive philosophical system encompassing logic, natural science, mathematics, and metaphysics. His monumental encyclopaedia, the Book of Healing, synthesised Aristotelian and Neoplatonic elements within an Islamic framework. His proof of the existence of God through the concept of the Necessary Existent proved influential on medieval scholasticism. The thought experiment of the Flying Man anticipated later European debates about self-consciousness and personal identity.

Al-Ghazali subjected rationalist metaphysics to systematic critique in his Incoherence of the Philosophers, arguing that the unaided intellect cannot establish the eternal creation of the world or individual immortality. His critique targeted specific claims of al-Farabi and Ibn Sina. This challenge sparked a major response from Ibn Rushd (Averroes), whose Incoherence of the Incoherence defended philosophical rationalism and Aristotelian method against theological objections.

Ibn Rushd became the principal transmitter of Aristotle to medieval Europe through his extensive commentaries, earning him the title the Commentator among Latin scholastics. Averroism, which held that philosophy and religion possess separate but compatible domains of truth, created space for autonomous rational inquiry. Thomas Aquinas engaged extensively with Ibn Rushd's commentaries while developing his own synthesis of Aristotelian philosophy and Christian theology.

Sufi philosophy offered an alternative path to knowledge through mystical experience and purification of the soul. Ibn Arabi formulated the doctrine of the unity of being, according to which all existence is a manifestation of a single divine reality. This concept influenced the poetry of Rumi and Hafiz and the development of Sufi brotherhoods across Central Asia and the broader Islamic world. The interplay between rationalist philosophy and mystical theology continues to shape Islamic intellectual traditions to the present day.
