The Silk Road was not a single road but a network of overland and maritime trade routes connecting China, Central Asia, the Middle East, and Europe for over a millennium. The term itself was coined by the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen in the nineteenth century, though the routes had existed since the Han Dynasty around 200 BCE. Silk was among the most prized commodities traded, but the routes also carried spices, glass, metals, textiles, and agricultural products in both directions.

Medieval trade along the Silk Road was organised through a relay system: merchants rarely travelled the entire length of the route but instead traded goods at key oasis cities. Samarkand, Bukhara, Merv, and Dunhuang served as major commercial hubs where caravans rested, restocked, and exchanged goods. The oasis cities of Central Asia grew wealthy from transit taxes and local crafts, developing into important centres of Islamic learning and culture during the eighth to twelfth centuries.

Beyond commodities, the Silk Road facilitated the transmission of ideas, religions, and technologies. Buddhism spread from India to China and Central Asia along these routes. Islam reached the Malay Archipelago through maritime Silk Road connections. The papermaking technology invented in China reached the Islamic world after the Battle of Talas in 751, and from there spread to Europe. Gunpowder, printing, and the compass followed similar westward trajectories.

The Black Death pandemic of the fourteenth century, which devastated Europe and the Middle East, also spread along Silk Road networks. Mongol expansion under Genghis Khan and his successors paradoxically stimulated long-distance trade by creating political stability across Central Eurasia under a single empire — the Pax Mongolica. European travellers such as Marco Polo could traverse the continent in relative safety during this period.

The gradual decline of the Silk Road in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries resulted from the rise of maritime routes connecting Europe directly to Asia around Africa. Portuguese explorers opened the Cape Route, which offered cheaper sea transport for bulk goods. The Ottoman conquest of Constantinople in 1453 disrupted overland routes and raised transit costs. Nevertheless, the Silk Road's legacy endured in the cultural, religious, and technological connections it had forged across Eurasia.
