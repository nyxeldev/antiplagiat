Quantum mechanics describes the behaviour of matter and energy at the atomic and subatomic scale, where the classical laws of Newtonian physics break down. The theory emerged in the early twentieth century from attempts to explain phenomena such as blackbody radiation and the photoelectric effect, which could not be reconciled with classical physics. Max Planck's proposal that energy is emitted in discrete quanta, and Einstein's extension of this idea to light, laid the groundwork for a revolutionary new framework.

The wave-particle duality of quantum objects is one of the most striking departures from classical intuition. Electrons, photons, and other quantum entities exhibit both wave-like and particle-like behaviour depending on how they are observed. The double-slit experiment demonstrates this duality: when electrons pass through two slits without observation, they produce an interference pattern characteristic of waves; when their path is measured, the interference pattern disappears and they behave as particles.

Heisenberg's uncertainty principle establishes a fundamental limit on the precision with which pairs of physical properties can be known simultaneously. The more precisely the position of a particle is determined, the less precisely its momentum can be known, and vice versa. This is not a limitation of measurement technology but an intrinsic feature of quantum systems. The uncertainty principle has profound implications for the stability of atoms, preventing electrons from spiralling into the nucleus.

Quantum superposition allows a quantum system to exist in multiple states simultaneously until a measurement is made, which collapses the superposition into a definite outcome. Schrödinger's wave equation governs the evolution of the quantum state over time. Entanglement correlates the states of separated quantum particles instantaneously, a phenomenon verified experimentally and now exploited in quantum computing and cryptography.

Applications of quantum mechanics pervade modern technology. Semiconductor devices — transistors, diodes, and integrated circuits — rely on quantum mechanical properties of electrons in solids. Lasers exploit stimulated emission, a quantum process. Nuclear magnetic resonance underpins MRI scanners in medicine. Quantum computers, using qubits that can be in superposition, promise exponential speedup for specific computational problems such as factoring large integers and simulating molecular systems.
