Nineteenth-century English literature is characterised by the rise of the novel as the dominant literary form, the social and moral concerns of the Victorian era, and a tension between Romantic idealism and Realist scrutiny of contemporary life. Writers such as Charles Dickens, George Eliot, Thomas Hardy, and the Brontë sisters produced works that interrogated class inequality, gender roles, industrialisation, and religious doubt. The serialisation of novels in periodicals made literature accessible to a broad middle-class readership and created new relationships between authors and their audiences.

Charles Dickens shaped Victorian fiction through his vivid portrayal of poverty and social injustice. Novels such as Oliver Twist, Bleak House, and Great Expectations depicted the suffering of the urban poor and the corruption of institutions, while using humour and memorable characterisation to reach wide audiences. Dickens's work had measurable social impact: his portrayal of Yorkshire boarding schools in Nicholas Nickleby contributed to parliamentary reform of educational conditions.

George Eliot (Mary Ann Evans) brought philosophical depth and psychological realism to Victorian fiction. Her novel Middlemarch, subtitled A Study of Provincial Life, interweaves multiple storylines to examine how social constraints limit individual aspirations, particularly for women. Eliot rejected the sentimentalism of earlier female fiction and engaged directly with contemporary debates in science, philosophy, and theology. Her writing reflects the positivist influence of Auguste Comte and Herbert Spencer.

Thomas Hardy's novels, including Tess of the d'Urbervilles and Jude the Obscure, confronted the tension between traditional rural life and the encroachments of industrialisation and modernity. Hardy's protagonists are often crushed by social conventions, coincidence, and indifferent fate. His pessimistic vision challenged Victorian optimism and drew attacks from conservative critics, leading Hardy to abandon fiction for poetry in the final decades of his career.

The late Victorian period witnessed the emergence of new forms of popular fiction: sensation novels exploiting mystery and crime, adventure stories by Stevenson and Haggard, and the detective fiction of Arthur Conan Doyle. Oscar Wilde's comedies and essays challenged Victorian morality through irony and aesthetic hedonism. The fin-de-siècle mood of decadence, symbolism, and aesthetic autonomy anticipated the Modernist revolt against Victorian literary conventions that would fully emerge in the early twentieth century.
