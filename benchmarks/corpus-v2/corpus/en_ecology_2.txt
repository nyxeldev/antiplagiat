Climate change refers to long-term shifts in global temperatures and weather patterns. While climate has always varied naturally, scientific evidence shows that human activities — primarily the burning of fossil fuels and deforestation — have been the dominant driver of climate change since the mid-twentieth century. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) has concluded with high confidence that global average surface temperatures have already risen by approximately 1.1 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels.

The greenhouse effect is the primary mechanism driving contemporary climate change. Greenhouse gases — carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, and water vapour — absorb outgoing infrared radiation from Earth's surface and re-emit it in all directions, trapping heat in the lower atmosphere. Atmospheric CO2 concentrations have risen from about 280 parts per million before industrialisation to over 420 ppm today. Methane, though shorter-lived than CO2, has a much stronger warming potential over a 20-year period.

The consequences of climate change are wide-ranging and interconnected. Sea levels are rising due to thermal expansion of ocean water and melting of glaciers and ice sheets, threatening coastal communities and low-lying island nations. Extreme weather events — heatwaves, droughts, floods, and intense hurricanes — are becoming more frequent and severe. Arctic warming is occurring at roughly four times the global average rate, accelerating permafrost thaw and releasing stored carbon in a feedback loop.

Biodiversity is severely threatened by climate change. Shifts in temperature and precipitation alter the distribution and phenology of species, disrupting ecological relationships such as pollination and predator-prey dynamics. Coral reefs, which support roughly a quarter of all marine species, face bleaching and mortality when ocean temperatures rise even slightly above their tolerance thresholds. The IPCC estimates that limiting warming to 1.5°C would prevent the loss of 70–90 percent of coral reefs; at 2°C, more than 99 percent would be lost.

Mitigating climate change requires rapid decarbonisation of energy systems, industry, transport, and land use. Renewable energy — solar and wind power — has become the cheapest source of new electricity generation in most of the world. Carbon capture and storage technologies can reduce emissions from industrial processes that are difficult to electrify. International cooperation through agreements such as the Paris Agreement provides a framework for national commitments, though current pledges remain insufficient to meet the 1.5°C target.
