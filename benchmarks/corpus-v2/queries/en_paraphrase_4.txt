The network of overland and maritime routes that connected China with the Mediterranean world for more than a thousand years was far more than a channel for commercial exchange. Cities situated along these routes became crucibles where religions, technologies, artistic styles, and philosophical ideas mingled and transformed. Buddhism, Islam, and Christianity all spread along these corridors. The compass, paper, and printing — all Chinese inventions — reached Europe via these networks. Conversely, glassmaking techniques, artistic motifs, and crops travelled eastward. The pandemic that devastated the fourteenth-century world also spread along these same pathways. When Portuguese navigators opened direct sea routes to Asia, overland trade lost its economic rationale, but the cultural connections forged over centuries proved more durable than the commerce that had first enabled them.
